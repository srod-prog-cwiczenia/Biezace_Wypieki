\section{Introduction}

Let us consider the following two spaces:
$\omega^\omega$ and $\omega^{\omega\uparrow}$.
It is obvious that they are homeomorphic by the standard
homeomorphism: 
$\psi \colon \omega^\omega \to \omega^{\omega\uparrow}$ defined
by the following:
$\psi(x)(n) = \sum_{k=1}^n x(k) + n$.

We can consider the dominating topology defined
on the space $\omega^\omega$ (by taking into accout 
the standard basis Hechler neighbourhoods:
$D(N, f) = \{g \in \omega^\omega \colon 
f \restriction N = g \restriction N \wedge
\forall_k f(k) \leq g(k)\}$.) In this way 
we obtain the standard dominating topology
on the space $\omega^\omega$. Using the
standard bijection $\psi$ we can "move" this
topology into the space $\omega^{\omega\uparrow}$.
  However, notice that the space 
$\omega^{\omega\uparrow}$ is a subspace
of the space $\omega^\omega$. So the precise
formulation of my question is like follows:
Does the topologies: The topology on 
the space $\omega^{\omega\uparrow}$
inherited from the usuall dominating topology
defined on $\omega^\omega$ (we treat here the
space $\omega^{\omega\up}$ as a subspace of the space
$\omega^\omega$) and the topology
also defined on the same space $\omega^{\omega\uparrow}$
but ``moved'' from the space $\omega^\omega$ (equipped
with the dominating topology of course) by the 
bijection $\psi$ are the same?
  
  This question is related to the two possibilities 
in introduction of the dominating topology in the space
$[\omega]^\omega$. Indeed, we can ``import'' the dominating topology
into the space $[\omega]^\omega$ by two possible ways:
The first one simply by the homeomorphism between 
$[\omega]^\omega$ and $\omega^\omega$ and the second one, by the
homeomorphism between $[\omega]^\omega$ and
$\omega^{\omega\uparrow}$ which is simply a subset of the space
$\omega^\omega$. In both cases we can move the topology 
by the functions. So we indeed obtain the two topologies.
Simply say that:

  We consider the canonical bijection:
$\Psi \colon [\omega]^\omega \to \omega^\omega$
and by this bijection we can move the dominating topology
from $\omega^\omega$ into the space $[\omega]^\omega$.

the second possibility is to consider the
cannonical bijecton 
$\Xi \colon [\omega]^\omega \to \omega^{\omega\uparrow}$
and then move the topology on $\omega^{\omega\uparrow}$
induced by the domination topology on $\omega^\omega$.
Are these two topologies the same? I think, the answer is
false. However, we can even have that the families of
Baire sets in our topologies are the same. 

OK, so let us formulate these two questions 
in a concise way:

\begin{question}
Let us introduce two topologies on the space
$\omega^\omega$. Define:
$\psi \colon \omega^\omega \to \omega^{\omega\uparrow}$ 
by the following:
$\psi(x)(n) = \sum_{k=1}^n x(k) + n$.
Then we can define the topology:
$\tau_1 = \{ \psi[U]\colon U \in \tau_D\}$, 
where of course $\tau_D$ denotes the dominating topology
on $\omega^\omega$.
However, consider $\omega^{\omega\uparrow}$ as a
subspace of the space $\omega^\omega$ and then 
consider the topology 
$\tau_2 = \{U \cap \omega^{\omega\uparrow}
\colon U \in \tau_D \}$.
The question is: Are these two topologies the same?
Or perhaps they have the same Baire sets?
Or perhaps they have the same meager sets?
\end{question}


\section{The Hechler topology}

Following \cite{LabedzkiRepicky1995} let us define for $s \in \finbaire$,
$f \in \baire$ such that $s \subseteq f$:
\[
  \Hechler{s}{f} = \lbrace x \in \baire\colon s \subseteq x \wedge \forall_{k \geq |s|} f(k) \geq x(k)\rbrace.
\]
\todo{
I suggest (as is done in the LabędzkiRepicky paper) using ($\omega$) left-associatively.}

Let us recall Lemma 1.1 from \cite{LabedzkiRepicky1995}:
\begin{lemma}
  The family
  \[\lbrace \Hechler{s}{f} \colon s \in \finbaire,
    f \in \baire, s \subseteq f\rbrace\]
  is a base of a topology.
  We call it {\it the Hechler topology} or {\it The eventually dominating topology}.
  Moreover, every base set $\Hechler{s}{f}$ is clopen in this topology.
\end{lemma}

Let us define the following bijection 
$\psi \colon \baire \to \incrbaire$, for
$x \in \baire$ define
$\psi(x)(n) = \sum_{k = 0}^n x(k) + n$, then
obviously $\psi(x) \in \incrbaire$.

Note that the inverse function $\ffid$ is given by the following
formula: 
\[
\ffid(\mu)(k) =
\begin{cases}
\mu(k) - \mu(k - 1) - 1 & \text{for } k > 0,\\
\mu(0) & \text{for } k = 0.
\end{cases}
\]

%%%Also we have: $(\ffid)^{-1}(f)(k) = \sum_{j=0}^k f(j) + k$.
Notice that we can also define $\psi(\tau)$
for a finite sequence $\tau\in\finbaire$ in the natural
manner, so $\psi\colon\finbaire\to\finincrbaire$ is a bijection.

On the set $\incrbaire$ we can define two different topologies:
the first is generated by the base sets of the form
$\psi[\Hechler{s}{f}]$
and we call it {\it the native Hechler topology} and
we denote it by $\HechlerNative$, and the second is generated by the basis
of the form $\incrbaire \cap \Hechler{s}{f}$ for
all $s \in \finbaire, f \in \baire, s \subseteq f$
such that $\incrbaire \cap \Hechler{s}{f} \not= \emptyset$. 
and we call it {\it the induced Hechler topology}
and we denote it by $\HechlerInduced$.

\begin{theorem} We have
$\HechlerInduced \subseteq \HechlerNative$ (so the ``native'' Hechler
topology is stronger).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Indeed, if $\Hechler{s}{f} \cap \incrbaire \not=\emptyset$
and if $\mu \in \Hechler{s}{f} \cap \incrbaire$, then
if $g \in \Hechler{\ffid(s)}{\ffid(\mu)}$ then
$\ffid(s) \subseteq g$, so $s \subseteq \psi(g)$.
Moreover, since $\ffid(\mu) \leq g$, we have
$\mu \leq \psi(g)$. This means that
$\psi(g) \in \Hechler{s}{\mu} \subseteq \Hechler{s}{f}$.
Thus $\mu \in \ffid[\Hechler{\ffid(s)}{\ffid(\mu)}] \subseteq
  \Hechler{s}{f} \cap \incrbaire$.
\end{proof}

\begin{claim}
  \label{claim:about-inf-0}
  Let $\INFZero = \lbrace x \in \baire\colon \exists_j^\infty x(j) = 0\rbrace$.
Then for each $\Hechler{s}{f}$ such that
$\incrbaire \cap \Hechler{s}{f} \not= \emptyset$
we have
$\ffid[\Hechler{s}{f} \cap \incrbaire] \cap \INFZero \not= \emptyset$.
\end{claim}
\begin{proof}
  Since $\incrbaire \cap \Hechler{s}{f} \not= \emptyset$
we know that $s \in \finincrbaire$, we may also assume that
$s \subseteq f$ and $f \in \incrbaire$. Find $\mu \in \incrbaire$
such that $\mu \in \Hechler{s}{f}$ and
$\exists_j^\infty \mu(j) + 1 = \mu(j + 1)$.
Then $\exists_j^\infty \ffid(\mu)(j) = 0$,
hence $\ffid(\mu) \in \INFZero$.
\end{proof}

\begin{claim}
For every $f\in \baire$ that doesn't terminate in $0$'s,
the image $\psi[\Hechler{\emptyset}{f}]$ doesn't
contain an nonempty $\HechlerNative$ - open set.
\end{claim}
\begin{proof}
Let $A= \{n:\;f(n) \not=0\}$ with the standard increasing enumeration
$(a_k:\;k<\oo)$.
Set $A_{Odd} = \{a_{2k+1}:\;k<\oo\}$ and
suppose that $M<\oo$ and $g\in\incrbaire$.
%suppose that $[M,g]$ is a basic open set.
%et $[M,g]\subseteq \psi([\emptyset,f])$ 
%be an arbitrary open set.
%For our purposes we may assume WLOG that $M$ is odd.
Define $h\in \Hechler{M}{g} \cap \incrbaire$ by 
\[
h(n) = \left\{ 
	\begin{tabular}{lll}
	$g(n)$ && if $n < M$\\
	$g(n) + 1$  && if $n \in A_{Odd} \setminus M$\\
	$g(n+1)$ && otherwise
	\end{tabular}
	\right.
\]
Suppose $n\in A_{Odd} \setminus M$. Then 
$h(n) = g(n) + 1$. And if $n \notin A_{Od}\cup M$,
then $h(n) = g(n+1)$.
In either case, $h(n) \ge g(n)$
and thus $h\in \Hechler{M}{g} \cap \incrbaire$.

For $n\in A_{Odd} \setminus (M+1)$,
we have 
$$
\inversepsi(h)(n) = h(n) - h(n-1) - 1 = g(n) + 1 - g(n-1+1) - 1 = 0 < f(n).
$$
%whenever $f(n) \not= 0$. We know that $f(n) \not= 0$
%for infinitely many $n$, and we will assume that 
%$f(n) \not= 0$ for infinitely many odd $n$. 
Thus $(\exists^\infty n)\;\inversepsi(h)(n) < f(n)$,
and it follows that $\inversepsi(h)\notin [\emptyset,f]$.
Therefore $h \in \Hechler{M}{g} \cap \incrbaire \setminus \psi([\emptyset,f])$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
  $\psi[\INFZero] \not\in \ND(\HechlerInduced)$. Moreover,
  $\psi[\INFZero] \not\in \meager(\HechlerInduced)$.
  \todo{Prove the latter using Claim \ref{claim:about-inf-0}.}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
  By virtue of Claim \ref{claim:about-inf-0}
we know that if   
$\incrbaire \cap \Hechler{s}{f} \not= \emptyset$
then
$\Hechler{s}{f} \cap \incrbaire \cap \psi[\INFZero] \not= \emptyset$.
So $\psi[\INFZero]$ meets each base neighbourhood of the
topology $\HechlerInduced$, hence 
$\psi[\INFZero] \not\in \ND(\HechlerInduced)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
$\HechlerNative \not\subseteq \HechlerInduced$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
It is easy to observe that 
$\INFZero \cap \Hechler{\emptyset}{\underline{\bf 1}} = \emptyset$.
By Claim \ref{claim:about-inf-0} we obtain that
$\ffid[\Hechler{s}{f} \cap \incrbaire] \not\subseteq \Hechler{\emptyset}{\underline{\bf 1}}$.
Since $\Hechler{\emptyset}{\underline{\bf 1}}$ is a Hechler base neighbourhood,
this proves the claim of the corollary.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:Hechler-induced-Ellentuck}
$\HechlerInduced \subseteq \EllentuckTopology$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  Let $\Hechler{t}{f}$ be a Hechler neighborhood and suppose
  that $x \in \Hechler{t}{f} \cap \incrbaire$. Then
  define: $s = \lbrace t(n) \colon n < |t|\rbrace \in \finsub$
  and $A = \lbrace x(n) \colon n \in \omega\rbrace \setminus s$.
  Let $B \in [s,A]$. Then $s \subseteq B \subseteq s \cup A$.
  We have: $s(k) = B(k)$ for $k < |t|$ and since
  $s \cup A = \lbrace x(n) \colon n \in \omega\rbrace$,
  $x(k) \leq B(k)$ for $k \geq |t|$. Hence
  $(B(k))_{k\in\omega} \in \Hechler{t}{f}$.
\end{proof}  
Let us summarize those inclusions in the
form of the diagram below:

\begin{tikzcd}
& \HechlerNative\\ 
 \HechlerInduced \arrow[ur] \arrow[dr] &  \\
   & \EllentuckTopology \\
\end{tikzcd}

\begin{theorem}
$\HechlerNative \not\subseteq \EllentuckTopology$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  Define $x \in \baire$ as follows:
  \[
    x(n) = 
\begin{cases}
10 & \text{ if } \neg(2 | n) \\
0 & \text{ if } 2 | n.
\end{cases}
\]
Let $\mu = \psi(x) \in \incrbaire$
and let $A = \lbrace \mu(k) \colon k \in \omega\rbrace$.
Let us fix $n_0 \in \omega$. Let us consider the
Ellentuck neighbourhood
$[A \cap n_0, A \setminus n_0] \subseteq [A]^\omega$.
We will show that
$[A \cap n_0, A \setminus n_0] \not\subseteq \psi[\Hechler{\emptyset}{x}]$.
We will construct $B \in [A]^\omega$ such that
$B \in [A \cap n_0, A \setminus n_0]$ (i.e.
$A \cap n_0 \subseteq B \subseteq A$)
and such that $(B(n))_{n\in\omega} \not\in

\end{proof}

We have also
\begin{lemma}
  Denote $\carre(k) = k^2$. Then
for each $s \in \finbaire$, $g_1,\ldots, g_L \in \baire$
we have
\[
  \psi [\EventuallyDifferent{s}{g_1, \ldots, g_L}]
  \not\subseteq \EventuallyDifferent{\emptyset}{\carre}\cap\incrbaire.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  We construct $x \in \EventuallyDifferent{s}{g_1, \ldots, g_L}$ by induction.
  Let us denote\linebreak $\hat{s} = \sum_{j=0}^{|s| - 1} s(j)$.
  Obviously, put $x \restriction |s| = s$. Next, for
  each $k \geq |s|$ we can find
  $a \in \lbrace 0, \ldots, L\rbrace \setminus \lbrace g_1(k), \ldots, g_L(k)\rbrace$
  and then define $x(k) := a$.
  Choose $K > |s|$ such that
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:warunek-carre}
  (K + 1)^2 > 2L + 2 + \hat{s} + (L + 1)K.
  \end{equation}
  We proceed in that way for $|s| \leq k < K$,
  and notice that we have
  $\psi(x\restriction K)(K - 1) = \sum_{j=0}^{K-1} x(j) + K - 1$.
  We are going to find $z, w \in \omega$ to define
  $x(K) = z$, $x(K + 1) = w$.
  We require that
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:warunek-pudelkowy}
    \begin{cases}
      z \not\in \lbrace g_1(K),\ldots, g_L(K)\rbrace,\\
      w \not\in \lbrace g_1(K + 1),\ldots, g_L(K + 1)\rbrace.
     \end{cases}
  \end{equation}
  We also demand that
  $\psi(x \restriction (K + 2)) (K + 1) = (K + 1)^2$.
  This mean that we want that
  \[
   \sum_{j = 0}^{K - 1} x(j) + z + w + (K + 1) = (K + 1)^2,
  \]
  so we want
  \[
    z + w = (K + 1)^2 - (K + 1) - \sum_{j = 0}^{K - 1} x(j).
  \]
  We have
  $\sum_{j = 0}^{K - 1} x(j) = \sum_{j = 0}^{|s| - 1} x(j) +  \sum_{j = |s|}^{K - 1} x(j)
  \leq \hat{s} + K \cdot L$.
  Note that
\[
  (K + 1)^2 - (K + 1) - \sum_{j = 0}^{K - 1} x(j) \geq
  (K + 1)^2 - (K + 1) - \hat{s} - K \cdot L > 2\cdot L + 1,
\]
by the condition (\ref{eq:warunek-carre}).
So $w = (K + 1)^2 - (K + 1) - \sum_{j = 0}^{K - 1} x(j) - z$
and we require that $z \leq  (K + 1)^2 - (K + 1) - \sum_{j = 0}^{K - 1} x(j) > 2 \cdot L + 1$.
Therefore we can find such $z$ which fulfills condition (\ref{eq:warunek-pudelkowy}).
Next we put $x(K) = z$, $x(K + 1) = w$ and then extend $x$ arbitrarily
(but still ensuring to avoid the functions $g_1, \ldots, g_L$).
\end{proof}
\begin{corollary}
$\EventuallyDifferentInduced \not\subseteq \EventuallyDifferentNative$.
\end{corollary}
